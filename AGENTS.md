# Upside Lab agent notes

**What this product is for, and what every rule below is in service of.** One
person, their own money, and learning to read it. Somebody arrives able to see
that a number went down and unable to say whether that was the whole market
having a bad Tuesday or the one company they were worried about, and the
product's job is to close that gap: state the figure, say what it is next to,
and teach the idea behind it at the moment they went looking. Two things fall
out of that and they are the tie-breakers when a rule below is ambiguous.
**Nothing is stated as fact that the reader cannot check**, which is why the
provenance mark exists, why a thin letter is not sent, and why no post-processing
moves the model's forecast. And **withholding is the second-best answer**: the
first is to show the thing and explain it. Hiding a screen because a reader
called themselves new is a decision to be argued for with a measurement, not a
default.

**How to read this file.** It is one long list of rules, each written the day
its bug was found, so its weight follows where the debugging went rather than
what matters most. Roughly a sixth of it is the bottom dock's motion. Read the
first fifteen bullets, which are the product; search the rest by the surface you
are about to touch. Broadly: the product and its data live at the top, then the
model and the copy rules, then auth, communities and the database, then the
scheduled mail, then the design system and the chrome from "The bottom dock" to
the end. `DESIGN_TOKENS.md` holds the full design account, `docs/` holds the
runbooks, and `docs/POLISH_PASS.md` is the standing audit checklist.

- **A share split is not a ninety per cent loss, and the app applies it the morning it happens.** Nvidia split ten for one on 10 June 2024: a hundred shares became a thousand at a tenth of the price and nothing was traded. Every price this app can read is post-split from that open, so a `portfell_holdings` row still saying a hundred is valued at a tenth of the truth, and it does not stay on one screen: that figure reaches **Pulse**, which reads it as a thesis breaking, the alerts, and the **Sunday letter**, which states its numbers as fact and would tell somebody in plain English that their week was a catastrophe. A reverse split does the same in the other direction and reads as a windfall. Migration `20260824120000_share_splits.sql` is the answer: `portfell_share_splits` is the ledger and its primary key makes applying one twice impossible, `portfell_split_checks` is the day's claim so one worker asks the provider, and `portfell_apply_split` does the arithmetic in one transaction. **The share count goes up and every per-share figure on the row goes down**: the buy price, the end of year target and any hand-set stock target, because all three are prices of one share and a $1,000 target on a stock that now trades at $100 is not a target anybody set. Fractions are kept rather than paid out, because Upside Lab holds fractional shares already. `fetchSplits` (`src/lib/market/yahoo.ts`) returns **null when it could not ask and an empty list when it asked and nothing happened**, and `applyDueSplits` hands the day's claim back on the first, because otherwise a provider having a bad minute costs a whole day of everybody's holdings being wrong. The cron is `0 15 * * 1-5`, which is after the opening bell in both halves of the year. Upside Arena has the same feature for the same reason (its migration `0027`), with one difference that is not a preference: Arena holds whole shares only, so it pays the leftover fraction out in cash at the post-split price, and this one does not have to.
- **Generic product direction (2026-08-12):** the app is moving toward being usable by people outside the family (friends, eventually strangers). Per-ticker "house" Call %/Stock-Target baselines and the forecast engine's hardcoded per-ticker price-target floors (old `CALL_PCT_BASELINES` / `STOCK_TARGET_BASELINES` / `TICKER_BASE_MULTS`) have been **removed on purpose** — Call % now comes purely from each ticker's own realized volatility (`src/lib/market/write-plan.ts`), and forecast paths are reasoned per-ticker by the model with only a modest, generic sector-shaped fallback (`src/lib/forecast-conviction.ts`) for gaps. Do **not** reintroduce ticker-specific hardcoded bias/targets — Martin's own investing opinions belong to him, not in code every user shares.

It is a floor, not a ceiling: a label passed as a prop or built in a variable is still on you, because widening it past markup text turns up hundreds of the identifiers this rename deliberately kept.
- **Three quarters of a room is below the fold, so most of it waits.** Measured at 390x800: Home 326 of 485 elements below (67%, 5.7 screens), Pulse 396 of 548 (72%, 7.9), Lab 202 of 250 (81%), Growth 565 of 713 (79%), Holdings 563 of 957 (59%, 8.1 screens). Two tools, and **which one applies is how far down the content is, not how big it is**. `BelowFold` (`src/components/BelowFold.tsx`) withholds the **mount**, so the children's effects and fetches wait too; it fires one whole screen early, and `rootMargin` in percent is a percentage of the root, so that is one screen of whatever device is reading. Holdings is its case: covered calls at 2,277px and the forecast at 4,081px, 532 of the room's 957 elements, both verified to render nothing until reached. **Growth is the instructive one and it took two goes**: wrapping its whole projection section did nothing, because the section starts at 1,218px with the fold at 800 and the lead reaching 1,600, so the observer fired at once and all 619 elements still rendered. Wrapped one level in it works -- the hero panel stays at 1,218 and every panel after it starts at 1,907 or lower, together 555 of the section's 618 elements -- and the room went from **713 rendered elements to 154**. **The test is the offset, not the size.** `defer-paint` (`content-visibility: auto` + `contain-intrinsic-size: auto`, globals.css) withholds the **work** instead, for the long list that cannot be sectioned -- Pulse is 498 elements in one 5,812px block. `auto` on the intrinsic size makes the browser remember each card's real height, so the scrollbar settles rather than lurching. **Never on anything with a `sticky` child**, since containment makes it a containing block. Measured over six hops at 4x, two runs each: 661 elements to 572, style 231 to 201ms, layout 55 to 37ms, JS 482 to 390ms. Rendered elements per room, before to after: Home 485 to 406, Growth 713 to **154**, Holdings 957 to **425**, Pulse unchanged at 548 but 7.9 screens to 6.0. Press-to-page-visibly-changed at 4x: Holdings **673ms to ~325ms, about half**, Growth ~520 to ~420. **Verified by scrolling every room top to bottom in quarters**: every section arrives, counts return to the originals, and no empty block over 200px is ever on screen -- a deferral that saves work and shows a hole is worse than no deferral. `below-fold.test.ts` holds the device-relative margin, the no-observer fallback, and the sticky ban.
- **A dock press says where it is going and the book shows it, because a transition shows you nothing until it is finished.** Screencast frame by frame at 4x CPU, tapping Growth, each frame diffed against a reference taken *before* the tap: for **600ms only about 2% of the pixels changed** -- the dock marker alone -- and then **23% of the screen swapped in a single frame**. That gap, not the total, is what a slow tap is. The cause is `startTransition`, which Next's `<Link>` navigates inside and whose defining behaviour is that the old screen stays until the new one is completely built. `src/lib/route-aim.ts` is the answer: the press handler that already aims the marker and prefetches the address also publishes it, `Dashboard` listens, and a **book** path is rendered immediately with an ordinary state update (never a transition, or it would wait the same way). `pathname` is still the truth and still settles it; the bet is refused for any room the book cannot draw, and gives up after `AIM_GIVES_UP_MS`. Measured press-to-page-visibly-changed: **1x 173/242ms to 149ms, 4x 624/717ms to 539ms** -- about a quarter at 4x and less at 1x, because it starts the work earlier rather than making it cheaper. **The lesson is the measurement**: three earlier rounds asked the DOM, the path and the profiler and all three said the navigation was fine; only the painted frames showed the reader six hundred milliseconds of nothing. When the complaint is about feel, diff painted frames, against a reference taken before the interaction.
- **Every room changes on the press now, not just the book's, and the measurement that said Circle was already fine was measuring the marker.** The bullet above put the press-time aim in `Dashboard`, which can only draw a book path, so it refused every bet that left the book: Circle, the Fund and Account each still waited out the whole commit. That went unnoticed because the obvious check is wrong in a way that always flatters. **A frame diff over the whole screen counts the dock's own marker as the page answering**: measured at 4x, the first changed frame after tapping Circle was **106ms**, which is the marker travelling, and the content above the dock did not change for **476ms**. Diff the content region, never the frame. `WorkspaceShell` reads the aim itself now (`shownPath = aimedPath ?? pathname`), so the room mounts on the press and shows its own loading state, and it refuses a path with no room of its own rather than blanking the screen for `/privacy`. Median of seven at 4x, content-only, before to after: **Home to Circle 266 to 203ms, Circle to Home 270 to 171ms**, with Home to Pulse flat at 114/119ms as the control that says the harness is honest -- a book tab was already aimed, so it must not move.
- **Aiming a link is `pointerdown` and the release takes it back, and `onClick` was measured and is not good enough.** A click handler reads as the safe way to do this, because the browser only fires a click when the press and the release land on the same element, so a drag can never be mistaken for a tap. It buys nothing: the handler runs in the same event as the navigation it is attached to, React batches the aim with the transition, and the aim gets no head start. Measured opening a circle at 4x, the room appeared at **514ms from a click and 457ms from the press**. So `aimOnPress` (`src/lib/route-aim.ts`) publishes on the press and stands down on a release that landed elsewhere, on `pointercancel` (which is what a scroll actually fires once the browser claims the gesture), and on the same `AIM_GIVES_UP_MS` backstop the dock uses. That guard is the whole reason it is a function rather than one line: a dock cell is a hard target, a row in a scrolling list is not, and flashing up a circle nobody asked for is worse than the 57ms. It refuses a middle click or a held modifier, which open the address in another tab. `route-aim.test.ts` covers all four ways it stands down, with hand-rolled window and element stubs, because this suite runs in node and there is no jsdom in the repo.
- **An aim that hides the element under the finger has destroyed the click that was going to navigate, so the press has to navigate itself.** Tapping a circle in the list took about four seconds and then put the reader back on the list. The bullet above aims the row on `pointerdown`, `WorkspaceShell` mounts the circle on that aim and hides the list room the row is in, and a browser dispatches a click only when the press and the release land on the same element. Measured in Chromium with a harness that hides the pressed anchor on `pointerdown`: with a mouse the click landed on `body`, with touch it landed on the new room, and in neither case did the anchor hear it. So `<Link>` never navigated, the URL never moved, and `AIM_GIVES_UP_MS` later the shell gave up on a bet nothing was ever going to settle -- four seconds of the right room, then the wrong one. The dock had met the same thing from the other side (pressing Circle hides the bar the finger is on) and its answer is the answer here: `aimOnPress` now judges the tap on the press's own evidence, released inside `AIM_TAP_HOLD_MS` without wandering past `AIM_TAP_SLOP`, and calls the navigator it was handed; the release's *target* is deliberately not consulted, because it is the one reading the aim itself made unreliable, and a click that does still arrive on the anchor is `preventDefault`ed so no room is entered twice. **The general rule, which is the dock's rule again: the moment a press changes the page, nothing the browser derives from what is under the pointer afterwards is evidence about that press.** Any new caller of `aimOnPress` whose room replaces the one it is pressed in must pass `router.push`; without a navigator the anchor's click is left to do the work, which is only right where the anchor survives the aim. The "Members · 1" on the same screen was fixed in the same pass: that number was the admin's count of people waiting to join, printed beside a noun that made it read as the roster of a fourteen-person circle. **It then had to be fixed a second time, and the tab carries no number at all now.** Printing the roster instead was true and still cost more than it was worth: the cells of a compact `Segmented` are `flex-1` with a zero basis, so they all take the width of the **longest** label, and "Members · 15" both widened every cell and crowded its own filled pill against the track. A count is not something a reader needs while deciding which tab to press, and it is already on the page that tab opens, so `membersCountLine` (`src/lib/members-count-line.ts`) prints "15 people, 1 waiting to join" under the Members heading and the tab says one word. The rule to carry: **a label in a segmented control is priced by the whole row, so measure the longest one and prefer moving detail onto the page it opens.**
- **A circle is 308 elements and three quarters of them start below the fold, so the two that matter wait.** Measured at 390x800 with eight members: 2.65 screens, the Today board beginning at 870px and Holdings you share at 1,347px, and between them **264 of the 308**. Both sit inside `BelowFold`'s own one-screen lead, so by the rule in the bullet above -- the test is the offset -- neither should qualify. They do, for a reason that bullet did not say out loud: **`BelowFold` starts closed, so its children are absent from the first render whatever the lead says**, and the observer opens them on the next task. The lead decides whether a reader ever sees a section arrive; being closed on the first render is what splits the work. `useState(false)` there is load-bearing and `below-fold.test.ts` now says so. The wrapper takes a `className` because a wrapped child is no longer a child of the original parent: `CircleHome` orders its sections in a flex column, and with the `order-*` left on the inner element both wrapped sections jump to the top. Measured opening a circle, median of six at 4x: **531ms to 332ms**, and page height, section offsets and element count all come back identical at 390, 430 and 1280 after a scroll to the bottom. **`/communities` itself needs none of this and was measured rather than assumed**: 161 elements over 2.28 screens with its biggest block above the fold, and the only block below it starting at 1,303px. Deferring that would save 23 elements and buy a hole. The League and Members sections are already deferred better than any of this, by the reader's own tab.
- **A fold that is placed automatically was built, measured per room, and taken back out, and the numbers are why.** Hand-placing `BelowFold` means reading an offset off one measurement on one device and typing a `reserve` in beside it, so the obvious improvement is to have the page shell do it: render a room whole the first time it is seen at a size, remember where each section landed keyed on route pattern plus bucketed viewport height, and start the ones past the fold closed next time. That was built (`PageMain`, `AutoFold`, `auto-fold.ts`), it worked, and **it cost more than it saved on most rooms.** Measured as one build with a per-room kill switch, so the only difference is behaviour rather than code, runs interleaved, press-to-content-change over the content region at 4x CPU: **Holdings 244ms with a fold against 411 without** (n=9, and the two sets barely overlap: 159-302 against 345-822), **Growth 324 against 359**, but **Home 303 against 290 and Pulse 314 against 292 -- both slower with it** -- and Account 337 against 347, Circle 269 against 276, both inside their own spread. A fold is a `useMemo`, a `useCallback`, an observer, a layout effect that writes to `localStorage`, and a wrapper element per section; on a room where nothing is below the fold to withhold, that is all cost. **Lab is the instructive one and is why n matters**: at n=5 it read as 91ms faster and at n=9 as 15ms, ranges fully overlapping -- and structurally it could never have been true, because both of Lab's sections start above the fold. Five samples of a hop whose spread is 100ms will invent an effect. **So the rule is the one this repo already had, now with a number against it: a fold pays where a room has a lot of elements starting well below the fold, and nowhere else.** Do not reach for an automatic version again without measuring the rooms it would touch; the whole experiment is in this session's scratchpad if somebody wants the code.
- **A room's chunk is warmed on idle, and the warm only works if it asks for the same module `dynamic` does.** Every tab panel in `Dashboard` and every room in `WorkspaceShell` is `next/dynamic`, which is right, and it means the FIRST tap on one pays a chunk fetch and a parse on the tap. Both files warm theirs on `requestIdleCallback` now. **Written as two separate `import()` expressions the bundler may give them different chunk groups**, and on the real build it did: the idle warm ran, 44 chunks came down in the first 400ms, and the first Pulse tap still fetched a 22KB chunk naming `PulsePage`. So there is **one named loader per panel and per room** (`loadPulsePage`, `loadBookRoom`, ...) referenced from both `dynamic()` and the warm. Measured on the real app at 4x CPU: **4 chunks fetched during taps became 0.** Lab and the covered-call panel stay behind the tier and options gates, since a reader who cannot reach them should not download them. **What this did not fix, measured, is the tap feeling instant**: timing the halves apart, `location.pathname` changes 400-600ms after the press and the room paints 70-240ms after that, so the App Router's own commit is most of it and happens before any of our rendering. `memo` on the mounted rooms was tried against it and reverted, no benefit past the noise. Variance on these hops is about ±150ms, so take medians; and never probe with `innerHTML.length` in a rAF loop, which serializes the subtree and inflates what it measures. Full account in `DESIGN_TOKENS.md`. **And the router commit was chased properly and is not a lever**: traced against a baseline (an idle 2.2s costs `style 0ms, layout 1ms, js 2ms` at 1x), a hop costs style 134-261ms and JS 284-586ms at 4x, and 17-55ms / 51-109ms at 1x, over a document of 591-1054 elements. Five hypotheses were measured and all were wrong: the main thread is **81% busy with no gaps over 8ms** (the earlier "idle" reading was a truncated trace plus a probe polling `innerHTML.length` per frame), the tap-time chunk is not on the critical path, `useDockPad` makes **zero** root-element writes during a hop, `memo` on the mounted rooms does nothing, and taking the dock's forced layout out moved the figure by less than the noise. What is left is the app building the destination panel: JS tracks the room's size and the style recalc follows up to 460 elements created or destroyed per hop. **The lever is per-panel render weight, not the framework.**
- **Every room is a path, and the book is many paths in one keep-alive room.** Home is `/`, then `/pulse`, `/lab`, `/growth`, `/alerts`, `/portfolio` and `/portfolio/<slug>`. They were one `useState` in `Dashboard` with `?tab=` written after it by `history.pushState`, which `usePathname` does not observe: the address bar reported on the app a beat late, Back was reconstructed from a private history stack, and both docks drew a real `<Link>` per cell and then cancelled it with `preventDefault`. `src/lib/book-routes.ts` is the only place that maps a tab id to a path (`hrefForTabId`) and back (`tabIdFromPath`), so an href and the reader of that href cannot drift. **One room, not one per page, and that is the whole design decision**: the book's pollers live in the `Dashboard` instance `WorkspaceShell` mounts, so they have to survive a walk between these pages; a room each would be a poller each, and a `WORKSPACE_SHOW_EVENT` cache check per tap, which is more traffic for the same screen. `workspaceRoomId` maps all of them to `"book"` -- and its `/portfolio` test sits **after** the `/upside-portfolio` one, because the Fund ends in the same word and a loose prefix would move it into the book with nothing failing. **The shell's layout effect had to be split and this is the trap to remember.** It saved and restored scroll and fired `WORKSPACE_SHOW_EVENT` on `[pathname, room]`, which was only ever a room change while the Dashboard wrote URLs behind the router's back; left alone, a tap on the dock would drop the reader at the offset saved the last time the book was left *and* reload the book whenever its cache was not fresh. `workspace-shell.test.ts` fails if the guard goes. What the flip deleted, and must not grow back: the URL write-back effect, the `popstate` listener, four history refs, the scroll reset that had to know whether a change came from a pop (App Router owns both directions now), `wantsHoldingsRef` and `pickInitialSheet` (a path is not stripped, so a cold-cache deep link simply re-resolves when the list lands), `stashOpenTab`/`takeOpenTab`, and `GO_HOME_EVENT`, which existed so a room whose URL said `/` could be told to show Overview anyway and cannot be true once `/` **is** Overview. Unchanged on purpose: the dock's cell count still comes from your data alone, never the route (`dock-stability.test.ts`).
- **The old `?tab=` URLs are answered forever, and the query is dropped rather than carried.** They are in browser histories, bookmarks and mail already delivered, so each 308s onto its path: `?tab=pulse` to `/pulse`, `?tab=portfolio&portfolio=<slug>` to `/portfolio/<slug>`, `?sheet=lab` to `/lab` (the meta reading comes first, or it opens a portfolio nobody has called "lab"), plus `/dashboard` and `/forecast` to `/` and `/compound` to `/growth`. **This is `src/proxy.ts`, not `redirects()` in `next.config.ts`, and the reason is measurable**: a config redirect appends the source's query to its destination with no setting to say otherwise, so `?tab=pulse` lands on `/pulse?tab=pulse`, leaving two spellings of one room in circulation, and `?tab=overview`, whose room is the root that query already sits on, redirects to itself forever. (Note this repo's Next has no `middleware.ts` -- `proxy.ts` is that file.) The table is `legacyRedirectPath` (`src/lib/legacy-urls.ts`), pure and tested apart from the request, and it reads `metaTabFromToken` rather than restating its spellings; `legacy-urls.test.ts` walks every token that function accepts and fails if one has no redirect behind it. New paths go in `PRIVATE_NOINDEX_PATHS` in the same commit that creates them, which is what gives them both the `X-Robots-Tag` and the `robots.txt` line. The old noindex header keyed on `?tab=` is gone, since a 308 answers before it.

- **The Research room opens on a price plan and four questions, because those are the two things a reader came for.** The order is what the room is: what the company is, then the levels, then the four questions, then where the estimate came from, then the accounts, then the argument. Everything below the four is the working behind one of them, which is why they sit third and not last. **The plan is a ladder of seven bands and it is arithmetic on two numbers already on the page**, an anchor and a step (`src/lib/company/plan-ladder.ts`). The anchor is the blended twelve-month estimate from the valuation panel, unmodified, and `anchorForCompany` says so out loud; a fund and a coin get no estimate anywhere in this app, so theirs is the middle of the year they have actually traded in and the panel's own subtitle changes to say that rather than promising a panel that is not on the page. The step is the share's own year, high against low, measured against that anchor: a name that swings twice as far gets bands twice as wide, held between a twentieth and a fifth, because a step under the floor makes a plan that fires twice a week and one over the ceiling makes a plan that never fires. The multiples are the reference ladders' own (an open band above 1.2 of the anchor, then 1.1, a hold band a step either side, then 0.8 and 0.7), and **the floor is not a step**: it is where the estimates stop describing the same company, about half the anchor, and it moves further down for a name that swings harder. **A ladder with no anchor is absent, never centred on today's price**, because a ladder centred on the price is this app saying the price is right, which is the one claim the room may not make. **Every level is the reader's and is stored as a multiple of the anchor rather than as a price** (`portfell_lab_state.ladders`, migration `20260906140000`), so a level set today still means what it meant when the estimates move; putting one back deletes the edit rather than freezing today's number. Two rules the copy learned the hard way: the read above the table says "the nearest level **on it**" until the reader has actually typed one, which is the `targetIsHandSet` lesson again, and the legal line is said once at the foot per the disclaimer rule rather than on every band. **The design was measured rather than reasoned about**, per this file's standing rule: rendered with the app's own CSS at 390 and 900 with every text node's line boxes diffed against every other's. Three faults only a screenshot found. A label positioned by its own centre clips at the ends of its track, so "You paid $420.00" hung outside the card entirely; the marks anchor to the near edge now and the tick stays at the true position. The `Card` primitive pads at `sm` and up, so a bare `p-0` lost to the responsive half and every hairline stopped 24px short of the card's edge. And **a pencil at the end of a row costs 44px of the band's own name under a coarse pointer**, which wrapped "Hold, nothing new" onto three lines at 390px: the whole row is the control now and the glyph is decoration from `sm` up, which is both a larger target and two lines shorter. **The four questions are `four-questions.ts` and every answer is a figure or it is absent**: where it sits names both yardsticks separately (its own year, and what a year of profit costs), what the price is assuming is the multiple worked backwards with no discount rate in it, the great-company question prints one quality figure and one price figure and **refuses to add them up**, because adding them up is a rating, and what would change your mind is a level and a date, both checkable, plus the model's own case against, named as the model's. A question the feed could not answer keeps its card, prints `n/a` and loses its accent rule, because a reader who cannot see that the third question went unanswered will assume it was answered well.
- **The four reference ladders were re-read properly, and two things fell out that the first pass got wrong.** All four share one shape (an open trim band, one step, a hold band two steps wide, one, one, then a wide accumulation band, then the floor), and the first version fitted only the two drawn on a company trading near its estimate, which both use a flat tenth of the anchor. **The floor is not a ratio.** The four floors sit at 0.46, 0.51, 0.30 and 0.54 of their own anchor, which is not a rule anybody chose: no rule produces four numbers that far apart on four ordinary companies. They are prices, and the reading that fits all four is **the lowest the share has actually traded in a year**, which is also the only level down there a reader can check. `ladderFloor` prefers it and falls back to the old ratio in exactly two cases, both of which stand down rather than bend the number: no year on file, and a low that is not clear of the accumulation band above it, which would end the ladder on two levels nobody could tell apart. The sentence carries the window it read (`windowSaid`), because a holding's ladder is drawn from the three months of closes the browser already holds and calling that "the lowest in a year" is a false sentence with a real number in it. **And a share trading a long way under the anchor gets a tighter ladder, which looked like an inconsistency in the references and is the interesting part of them.** The two drawn on deeply discounted companies use steps of 3.25% and 3.69% against the others' flat 10%, with accumulation bands 18.6 and 9.6 steps wide. The bands are doing a different job in the two cases: near the anchor each step is another slice of a live decision, and a long way under it every price between the floor and the bottom of the ladder is the *same* decision, so cutting that stretch into tenths is precision the situation does not have, while the top, where the trimming levels are, is what this reader will actually meet if they are right. So `FAR_BELOW_STEP_FACTOR` takes a third of the ordinary step when the price is under the ordinary ladder's own bottom, and the stretch below becomes one band. **`MIN_STEP` does not apply in that regime and that is the point rather than an exception**: that floor exists so a ladder does not fire twice a week, and it cannot, because the price being nowhere near those bands is the condition that put it there. The regime is judged against the *ordinary* step and never iterated, or the tightened ladder's own accumulation band would answer yes for almost any price under the anchor and the two regimes would have no boundary. It is said on the panel as well as behind the mark, because a ladder 3.5% wide sitting next to one 11% wide reads as a fault otherwise.
- **The step is a tenth of the anchor with two fifths of the name's own swing on top, not a number proportional to volatility.** The first version scaled the step straight off the year's range, which pulled an ordinary large company out to 15.7% on nothing but a wide year. The references are the evidence against that: they put an identical flat 10% on two companies whose volatility is nothing like each other's, so the source plainly treats it as a constant. `SWING_WEIGHT` is the compromise and it matters more since the floor started coming from the year's low, because the same year would otherwise set both the width of every band and the depth of the floor and one bad month would widen the ladder twice over. The bounds came in with it: `MIN_STEP` 8% and `MAX_STEP` 14%, close either side of the reference tenth, since a step wandering between a twentieth and a fifth is not the same ladder any more. **`plan-ladder.test.ts` holds all four published tables and fails if a change stops reproducing them**: with each reference's own floor as the year's low and a plausible year's high, every edge lands within 4% of the published one and every floor is exact. A change here that breaks them is a different model and needs its own argument.
- **A price plan is the reader's, and this app is not an adviser, so the distinction is carried by the design rather than by a sentence.** The bands are named for what that reader decided to do at that price, which reads as instruction and is not one: the panel is headed "Your price plan", every level is editable in place with a reset beside it, the provenance mark opens onto a multiplication with no model and no view of this company in it, and `ADVICE_DISCLAIMER_SHORT` is at the foot. The ladder alert (`buildLadderAlerts`) repeats a level being reached and nothing else, and it says which kind of plan it is: the plan **you set** where the reader typed a level, and the plan **this app worked out and you have not changed** where they did not. Only the ends of the ladder are loud enough to alert on, since a band in the middle is where a price ordinarily sits and an alert that fires while nothing has happened is one a reader learns to swipe past. The id carries the band for the reason the borrowed-money alert's carries its tier: a dismissal is per id, so one id for every level would silence the floor because somebody waved off a trim in March. Nothing on this path may grow a verdict word, a score, or a sentence beginning "you should"; `plan-ladder.test.ts` fails on one in the read at the top.
- **A portfolio is one picture, and the picture is a bar per band with one block per holding.** One name's "trim a little" is $456 and another's is $1.80, so prices cannot share an axis; every band is a multiple of that name's own fair value, so the **band** can be. `band-map.ts` files every holding into its band and `BandMap.tsx` draws the ladder as seven rows, each carrying a bar whose length is **how much of the reader's money is priced in that band**, split into a block per holding sized by what it is worth. That reading is the reason for the design: a portfolio mostly priced under fair value looks different at a glance from one mostly priced over it, and no list of prices says so. **The design before it was a scatter and every fault it had was one fault**, which is that its proportions moved with the portfolio: lanes were drawn as tall as their band was wide and taller again when crowded (`LANE_WEIGHTS`, `stackDepth`, `EMPTY_LANE_SCALE`), so an ordinary book put a giant "hold" lane between hairline-thin empty ones and the same ladder read differently on two portfolios; chips were *placed* by a collision pass rather than laid out, so they still drew through each other on a crowded book; chip size was scaled up to fill the room, which at six holdings on a wide screen reached 2.4x and made pills that looked broken; and the outermost chip's edge landed flush on the plot's own border. All of that machinery is deleted rather than tuned. **Three rules replace it and each is load-bearing.** A row is a **fixed height whatever anybody owns**, including the rows with nothing in them, which is what makes the ladder's shape a constant: verified at 360, 390, 430, 768, 1280 and 1440 on a 1, 6 and 14 holding book, every row is identical. Blocks are **laid out, never placed**, so two names cannot overlap however many are in one band. And **colour is one dot**: a tinted pill plus a tinted border plus an accent ring is three signals over one object, which is what read as muddy against the near black, so the gain and loss pair is spent on a 6px dot and the warm accent marks only a name at an end of its plan. An empty band is **drawn and drawn quieter**, at 45% with a hairline where its bar would be, because the shape of the whole ladder is half of what a reader came for but an empty band does not need the same ink as a full one. **How many names fit in a bar is a fact about the device, not about the portfolio**, so it is measured in the view (`blocksThatFit`) rather than capped in the model: six blocks at their own minimum width is 411px, which a 390px phone does not have, and a laptop has room for a dozen. **Folding is decided by room, never by size alone, and that correction is worth keeping.** The first version dropped anything under a few per cent of the portfolio once there were more than eight holdings, which read well on a crowded band and was nonsense on a quiet one: measured on a book with one holding at 69%, the band holding two names worth 1.5% and 0.2% folded **both** of them and drew "+2 small" over a bar with room for six, so a reader could not see what was in their own band without hovering it. `foldToFit` folds only when the bar has run out of slots; size still decides *which* name goes, because the blocks are ordered biggest first and folding takes from the end. **A holding at an end of its own plan jumps that queue and is kept however small it is**, since it is the row this picture exists to surface and exactly the one an ordering by size throws away first. What folds says so: a "+N" block carrying the count, the names behind it for a screen reader, and what they come to together. `TINY_SHARE` survives only to choose the word "small" over "more". **A block grows by its share of ITS OWN BAND, never of the portfolio, and getting that wrong is invisible in the markup.** Flex hands out only the **sum** of the grow factors when that sum is under one, and a band's portfolio shares always are: growing each block by its portfolio share made a band holding 55% of the money fill 55% of its own bar and leave the rest empty, so the length a reader actually saw went as the **square** of the share, and it was right only for whichever band happened to be the fullest. Measured on a real book, three names in a 294px bar all sat at their 72px floor with 71px of bar unfilled beside them, which reads as a slightly short bar and is a wrong figure. `barShares` is the arithmetic and `band-map.test.ts` asserts the factors sum to one, including on a portfolio worth nothing. Verified in the browser: every bar is filled to the pixel at 360, 390, 430, 768, 1280 and 1440 on four portfolio shapes, and the lengths are exactly proportional (55% drew 753px, 21% drew 288, 18% drew 246, 6% drew 82). **A phone has no hover, so a block wide enough to carry its own share prints it** (`SHARE_AT_PX`): the tooltip with the price and the share is a laptop's privilege, and the first version left a phone reader with a ticker and a coloured dot. **A zone's heading is a banner, not a caption, and everything in a row is consistent with everything else in it.** Three faults, all the same fault, which is a reader unable to tell why one thing looks different from another. The zone headings were mono caps in muted grey on a 9% wash, the same voice every other small label in the app uses, so the three groups were something a reader had to look for: the heading is a filled bar now, carrying the zone's own colour in a rail and its name in that colour at sentence size and weight, with the share beside it. Some blocks carried a warm tint and others did not, which was the accent for "an end of the ladder" and reads as arbitrary once the zones carry meaning of their own, so it is gone: where a name sits **is** the row it is in. And some blocks printed their share while others did not, because only the wide ones had room, which is a difference with no cause a reader can see: every block prints it, and the block's own minimum width is the width that fits it. **What a band means lives behind the mark beside its name**, not in a column of its own: the range is a fact a reader needs once and then knows, and a column printed it on all six rows for the life of the account. That also retired `flexWidths`, which existed only to work out which blocks had room for a share. **The six rows read as three answers, and that grouping is what makes the picture scannable.** Six rows of near-identical grey is a table a reader parses rather than sees, and what they are actually looking for is whether their money is priced above, around or below what its companies look worth. The rows sit under those three headings, each zone carries its own wash and a full-strength rail down its left, and the heading takes the zone's share of the portfolio. **Warm above and cool below, never the gain and loss pair** (`--zone-warm`, `--zone-cool`, added to `DESIGN_TOKENS.md` before they were used): every block on these rows already carries a gain or loss dot, so tinting the zone emerald and rose would put two meanings on one pair and a holding in profit would sit in the colour of a loss. The middle zone stays neutral, because "around fair value" is not a direction. **The two washes are balanced by measurement, not by matching the numbers**: the same alpha of the cool hue lands further from the near-black field than the warm one does, and a saturated blue reads brighter still than its luminance says, so an even pair gave a cool zone that shouted over the warm. Sampled off the rendered page, 0.06 and 0.11 measured chroma 8.0 and 13.1 against the warm zone's 5.0 and 8.0; 0.038 and 0.068 land on 5.0 and 8.1, with the cool a touch darker to pay for the Helmholtz-Kohlrausch effect this repo already balances its ambient lobes on. **A share is never rounded into or out of existence**: `percent(0.004, 0)` is "0%", which beside a bar with a name in it states as fact that a drawn holding is worth nothing, and "none" is the same lie the other way, so a band under half a per cent prints "<1%" in the column and "less than 1%" in a sentence. Above the ladder sit three tiles, each a figure already on the page: how much of the portfolio is priced around fair value with the split below and above it, which names have reached a level the reader set (named as the reader's own, split into the trimming end and the adding end, and read off the app's own `ACTIONABLE_BANDS` so it cannot disagree with the alerts), and what the biggest holding is doing.
- **The band map's own sentence about a reached level must say whose level it was, and it did not.** The bands are labelled in the plan's own imperative voice, "trim most of it", "add a lot", and what keeps six imperatives printed beside somebody's real tickers honest is that the plan is the reader's. A default this app worked out is not theirs. The summary tile said "MU, BMNR at a level **you set** for adding" whatever the truth was, which is false on any reader who has never opened a ladder, and it is the one sentence that turns a computed default into this app's instruction. `readySaid` (`band-map.ts`, pure and tested) says "Levels you set." only when every reached level was edited, "Levels this app worked out, which you have not changed." when none was, and neither when they are mixed. The alerts have drawn this distinction since they were written (`alerts.ts`, `BandAlerts.tsx`); the picture above them did not until somebody asked whether the table could be flagged. `band-map.test.ts` fails if it claims a level was the reader's when it was not, and on an instruction word in that sentence, which is the guard `value-glance.test.ts` already gives the valuation room and this path had none of.
- **A BAND SAYS WHERE THE PRICE IS. IT DOES NOT SAY WHAT TO DO.** The bands were imperative for most of this app's life: "Trim 60%+", "Consider a trim", "Half a starter", "Full position", "Out of it", and then a tidier set of the same kind, "Trim most of it" through "Add a lot". The argument for that was always that the words are the **reader's**: a plan is a decision made in advance, so the label completes "at this price, my plan says ...", and this app is only printing it back. **That argument has a hole, and it is the defaults.** Most readers never open a ladder, so what they actually see is six imperatives this app computed, set beside their own tickers, on a screen anybody may now reach; the instruction is the app's however the label is framed, and a frame is a poor thing to rest on when the downside is somebody reading "trim most of it" as advice. So they describe the price: **A long way above, A little above, Close to fair value, A little below, A long way below, Below its whole year**. Each is a fact about a number, checkable against the figures printed beside it, and the decision is left where it belongs. **Do not put a verb back in here**, and note that the sentences quoting a band elsewhere (`alerts.ts`, `BandAlerts`, `ladderRead`) survive the change unaltered because "the band your plan calls X" is true of a descriptive name too. **Three bands about adding were two too many**: "Add more" and "Add a lot" were one step apart and nobody could say which was which, so `full` was merged into `full-aggressive` and the ladder is six bands, symmetric one step either side of the middle. That costs one of the four published reference ladders' six levels, and `plan-ladder.test.ts` names the level it no longer draws and still holds every other one to within 5%, because dropping a level is a decision and drifting off the rest is a bug. **`full` stays in `LadderBandId` and is gone from `EDGES`**, because it is still sitting in saved levels and dismissed alerts: a reader who set a level on it loses that one level rather than having their whole plan refuse to parse, and nothing may put it back without merging those saved edits somewhere first. **The ids never change with the wording** (`trim-most`, `starter`, `exit` ...), which is what a saved level, a dismissal and an alert are keyed on, so every renaming here has been a wording change needing no migration. **Merging the model further was considered and refused**: each band is one step wide and those widths are what reproduce the references. `bandRangeSaid` prints the distance and takes a `direction` flag rather than always saying "above fair value" or "below fair value": where a column header or a grouping heading already says which side it is on, repeating it printed "fair value" six times down one column and left the distance, the only part that changes, as the tail of a sentence. It stays approximate on purpose, since the step is each company's own between `MIN_STEP` and `MAX_STEP`, so one printed range is the shape of the ladder rather than a promise about any one holding.
- **A holding with no target of its own is anchored on its own trading range, not on a shape grown from today's price.** `resolveTickerForecastPath` answers with the reader's own end of year price where they set one and otherwise with a path grown from **today's price** by a multiple that depends only on the kind of business. That is fine as a forecast shape and useless as an anchor: every holding in a theme is then the same fraction of its own anchor, so the ladder says where the price sits against a rule of thumb about that theme, and the map of them would order a reader's holdings by how volatile they are while looking like it ordered them by how cheap they are. So `anchorForHolding` takes a target only when somebody chose it, and otherwise the middle of the range this name has actually traded in, saying outright that this is a plan about the price and not a claim about worth. It is the same fallback a fund and a coin get on the company page, for the same reason.
- **One builder makes a holding's plan, and three surfaces read it.** `holdingLadders` is called by the map on the holdings page, by the list on Home and by the alert builder, and the Research room's own ladder for a holding you own (`YourHolding`) is built from it too, rather than keeping a second copy of the same arithmetic. The day those stop agreeing is the day a reader is told on one screen that a level was reached and on another that it was not. `ACTIONABLE_BANDS` is the same rule for the same reason: the ends of a ladder are what the list shows and what the alerts fire on, and the middle is deliberately absent from both, because a price in the band a plan calls "hold, nothing new" is the ordinary case and a list of those is the portfolio again, which Home already has. Home's card names the band, the price and the level, and pressing a row opens that company's page: **it says which names are worth opening, never what to do about them**, and the legal line is at its foot.
- **The same ladder is on every holding, and it is the same function.** A holding's anchor is its own end of year target, the reader's where they typed one and the shared path's where they did not, because that figure is already printed a few inches above it on the Research room's own price plan, where `YourHolding` draws what they paid as a second, quieter mark on its own band, since that is the context a holder has and a researcher does not — an anchor a reader cannot see is a plan they cannot argue with. That mark is only drawn where the listing is quoted in dollars, because a buy price is stored in dollars and the ladder is in the listing's money, and a euro cost basis on a dollar ladder lands in the wrong band. The swing comes from the price history the browser already holds, which is about three months rather than the feed's year, so `stepFor` takes the window in words and the sentence behind the mark says three months: a step read off three months and described as a year is exactly the quietly wrong sentence this app does not print. `anchorForHolding` and the three month window stay, because `Dashboard` still runs the same builder over every holding to feed the alerts, so a level a reader saw on one screen is the level that wakes them on another.
- **A holding opens Research, and there is no drawer any more.** Pressing a company in the holdings table used to open `TickerDrawer`, a side panel restating a little of the company room: a price path already in Forecast, the same price plan, a covered call line already in its own panel, what an ordinary day looks like, and a box for the reader's own reason. Everything in it was either on another screen already or a thinner version of one, and having two rooms for one company meant the command palette offered both and neither answered the other. The drawer is deleted, and a press on a holding or on the ticker in the desktop table goes to `/stock/<ticker>`. `YourHolding` (`src/components/company/YourHolding.tsx`) is what a holder gets there: shares, what was paid, what it is worth, the gain, above the price plan, because somebody who owns the company came to think about a position rather than to meet a company. It is drawn **even when the feed's page failed**, since what a reader owns is theirs rather than the provider's. **It asks nothing, and that is the rule to keep**: no box for a written reason and no score for how sure they are, because the app does not ask a reader to grade their own conviction and a panel that did would be the one place left doing it (`test-invariants.ts` fails on either). **Every figure on it is in dollars and the panel says so**, which is the `PositionFitCard` rule again: a buy price is stored in dollars and `facts.price` is in the listing's money, so subtracting one from the other on a euro listing would be a hundred per cent wrong, and the same test decides whether the ladder below may draw what the reader paid as its second mark. Removing a holding moved to the foot of the phone card rather than into the company room, which has no portfolio in it to remove one from; the desktop table's row bin is unchanged.
- **The Research room's price is live and the page refetches itself when the price has moved past the writing around it.** The page is the feed's whole record of a company plus headlines plus a written brief and it is expensive; the price is not, and the price is what decides which band of the plan the reader is looking at. So the room polls the quote on the same curve as the rest of the app (`quotePollMs`, tight at the bell and slack overnight) and the page's own figure stands until the first live one lands. Past a 5% drift the whole page is refetched rather than just the number, because at that distance the estimates were reasoned against a different price and the headlines that moved it are the ones the reader came for. It is the same rule the shared brief cache applies on the server, checked in the room so somebody who leaves it open through a results day is not reading yesterday's argument under today's price.
- **Somebody can look up a company they do not own, and the whole design of that room is that no sentence in it is unfalsifiable.** Everything else in this app assumes you already hold the thing: Pulse explains a move in a holding, the forecast projects holdings, the recall deck asks about your own rows, and the watchlist could take a name and then do nothing with it but mention it in an email. The complaint the room answers is the one people actually make, which is that finding out what a company is takes a level of reading nobody has time for and the financial half is written for somebody who already understands it. `/stock/<ticker>` is a room of its own (`workspaceRoomId` gives each company its own keep-alive pane, capped at `MAX_STOCK_ROOMS`; a shared id would show the reader the last company while the new one loaded, which is worse than a spinner because it is wrong rather than empty), and Lab's fifth tab is the front door: a search field, the companies you looked at, the ones you are watching, the ones you own. **The split that makes it safe is absolute and it is enforced in three places.** Every figure comes from a provider and lands in a field of `CompanyFacts`; a feed that answered with nothing yields `null`, which reads as `NO_VALUE`, because "we do not have this" is true and a plausible-looking guess is not (`company-facts.test.ts` walks every reading against a company the feed carried nothing for). **Every heading in this room is the ordinary financial name for what is under it, and the first version had it wrong in both directions**: panels were called "What you pay, and what it looks worth" with cells reading "Is the profit arriving" and "What the price is assuming", which nobody says out loud. Vagueness is not simplicity: a reader who does not know what a price target is learns nothing from a riddle, and a reader who does has to decode one to find a figure they could have read at a glance. It is Valuation, Share price, 12 month estimate, Analyst target range, Earnings per share, Key financials, and the plain sentence underneath does the explaining. **The same applies figure by figure**: the labels were paraphrases ("Whether they make money"), so somebody who knows what a profit margin is had to decode a riddle to find one, and somebody who does not was left unable to recognise the word anywhere else, which is the exact cost AGENTS.md already records for the outright ban. **And every figure is set against something real rather than a rule of thumb where one exists**: the feed's `indexTrend` publishes the S&P 500's own expected earnings growth, so a company's 104% sits beside "S&P 500 · 15%" as a chip on the same line, and a margin is measured against the same company's margin four years ago rather than against a table. The model is handed those figures and the headlines and asked **which of them matter**, never for facts of its own: every point it makes carries a citation naming a reading id, a headline index, or the company's own description, and `resolveCompanyBrief` deletes any point whose citation does not resolve, including one citing a figure that came back `n/a`, which is the nastiest case because the citation resolves and the number is not there. The count of deleted points is **printed for the reader** rather than swallowed, since a run that lost half its points deserves less trust from the person reading what is left. And the page ends on the door out: the articles with the publisher and the date on each, the company's own filings, and the feed's own page, so the answer to "why should I believe this" is never "because it is on the screen". **This app certifies nobody as trustworthy.** There is deliberately no allowlist of respectable publishers and no ranking of one against another; that judgement would be an editorial line handed to every reader in the product, decided by whoever last edited a list in a file. The publisher's name and the article's date, at the same size as the headline, are the honest version of the same help. Prompt injection through a filing or a headline is handled structurally rather than by a warning: a smuggled instruction still has to produce a point that names something the reader can open.
- **What a company is worth is several estimates, each showing its working, and never a verdict, and the horizon is what makes them right or nonsense.** `fair-value.ts` runs what it can on the figures that arrived: the analysts' twelve-month average (weighted by how many published, because one analyst is one spreadsheet and twenty is a consensus), earnings against the market's own long-run multiple, and the same with the growth rate put back in. Two blends come out, today's figures and a year ahead, each method listed with the one assumption it rests on **and a working line naming its actual inputs**, so a professional can reproduce the number and a beginner can still read the sentence above it. That layering is the rule for the whole room: plain first, exact second. **The fault that made the first version produce nonsense was the horizon, and it is worth knowing because nothing about it looks wrong.** Every method ran on trailing earnings, so a company whose profit is about to double came out at a fraction of its share price with the arithmetic perfectly sound: the profit being paid for has not happened yet, so of course last year's does not justify the price. Measured on AMD in September 2026 that was $78 a share against $309, and a blended "worth" of $148 against a price of $478. The earnings figure is now the analysts' own estimate for the year in question (`earningsTrend`, which the feed publishes and nothing here was reading) and the label says which year it is. **A second method went the same way as the first, and the rule they share is the one to carry.** "Priced like the average company" multiplied a year's earnings by the market's long-run 20x. Applied to a company growing earnings at 104% a year it asks what the company would be worth if it were an average one and prints the answer as what it is worth: on AMD, $151 a share against a price of $478, dragging the headline figure to $223. A page carrying a number like that does not read as conservative, it reads as fabricated, and it costs the credibility of every honest figure beside it. **Arithmetic being correct is not the same as a method applying**, and a method whose premise does not hold for this company must not appear in a blend of what that company is worth. Nothing was lost: the figures panel already prints this company's multiple next to the market's as a comparison, which is a statement about how it is priced rather than a claim about what it is worth. With it gone there is **one estimate, twelve months out**, because every surviving method is forward by construction (an analyst's target, a multiple of next year's earnings, the model's path) and two headline figures where only one is defensible is worse than one. `fair-value.test.ts` fails if anything reappears that prices an AMD-shaped company anywhere near the market's yardstick. **Three more rules, each learned from a name it got wrong.** No method disappears: one landing more than three times from the median is dropped and still listed, greyed, with the reason, because a silently dropped estimate is the forecast floor's mistake in a new room. Nothing is nudged towards the current price in either direction, or the number always agrees with the market and says nothing. And no output contains a verdict word: not cheap, not expensive, not undervalued, no rating, because the conclusion is the reader's.
- **The model's number is read at the horizon the panel promises, and nothing else about it is adjusted.** The shared forecast path prices calendar year ends, so its first point is the close of *this* year, and the valuation panel is headed "what each method puts it at in twelve months". Those coincide for about one day a year. Read on 6 September the first point is under four months out, and it was printed next to analyst targets covering three times as long, so the model came out looking timid for a reason that had nothing to do with what it said: on Meta it landed at $691 against a $616 price while the analysts' twelve-month average was $754. That is the horizon fault this file already records against a method run on trailing earnings, in a new place: the arithmetic was sound and the clock was wrong. `modelTwelveMonthPrice` interpolates between the first two points of the path, in log space because a price compounds rather than adds, and the working line names both points and the date it read between them; with only the first point available the method says out loud that it is quoting a nearer horizon. **It is a horizon correction and not a house view, and the test that proves it is the falling one**: a path that declines through next year comes out *lower* here, not higher. Do not reach for this as a way to make a forecast more optimistic. The rule the `MARGUS_PERSONA` and forecast-floor bullets state is unchanged and covers this file too: no floor, no lift, no named strategist, no minimum a model must clear, and no bullishness compiled into anything every reader shares.
- **There is no discounted cash flow, and that is a decision rather than an omission.** One was built, shipped in the first draft, and taken out, because it was confidently wrong rather than uncertain and the reason is in the data. The only cash figure this feed supplies is `freeCashflow`, computed **after interest and debt service**, which is not what anybody else means by free cash flow and is wrong by a factor of two or three on ordinary companies: measured on 2026-09-05, Nvidia reported $41.8 billion of it against $134.4 billion of operating cash flow, and Coca-Cola $5.2 billion against $16.3 billion. Ten years projected off a starting number that far out, discounted, produces a figure with a great deal of arithmetic behind it and no relationship to the company; it valued Coca-Cola at $11 a share against a price of $88. There was a second bug on top, and it is the one anybody re-adding this will hit: **a stream that has already paid its interest is an equity stream, so adding net cash and subtracting debt on top counts the balance sheet twice**, which charged Coca-Cola $40 billion of debt it had already serviced. The honest fix needs capital spending, and `cashflowStatementHistory` comes back empty from this feed (the library says so in a warning). What replaced it is `impliedGrowth`, the same question backwards and from figures that are solid: the annual earnings growth needed, over five years, to bring the multiple back to what the market ordinarily pays. It assumes no discount rate and no terminal value, it is the thing a professional actually asks, and it is the fairest framing a company that looks expensive can get, because the price is not wrong, it is a bet, and this is the size of the bet.
- **A rule of thumb that has nothing to say must stand down rather than floor.** The growth-multiple method allows a company compounding earnings at 20% a year roughly a 20 times multiple, which is the oldest heuristic in the business and a **growth-company** heuristic. Floored at 8, it valued Coca-Cola, growing at about 7%, at eight times earnings, which is $26 against a share price of $88 and is not an estimate anybody would defend: a steady company growing with the economy is ordinarily priced near the market's own multiple for reasons this rule knows nothing about, dividends and predictability among them. It refuses below 12% now, or below the market's own expected growth where that is higher, and what it does above that line is **anchor on the market rather than on the growth rate**. The old form was `multiple = growth rate`, which is the heuristic as it is usually said and which breaks in both directions: it valued Coca-Cola at eight times earnings, and it valued **Meta, growing earnings at about the same pace as the S&P 500, at twelve times**, which is a multiple no large profitable company has traded at in a decade and which a reader can see is nonsense at a glance. A number that is visibly wrong on a company everybody knows costs the credibility of every honest figure beside it, which is the same argument that deleted the two methods above. So it is `MARKET_EARNINGS_MULTIPLE + (growth - market growth) * GROWTH_PREMIUM_PER_POINT`, capped at `GROWTH_RULE_CEILING`: a company growing at the market's pace gets the market's multiple, and each point of growth above the market buys half a turn more. It is deliberately **not** a bullish adjustment and must not become one: it is symmetric about the market, it still stands down entirely below the floor, and the cap still binds the top. `marketPct` comes from the feed's own index figures, never a constant typed in here, so the comparison moves with the market. There is no house market view anywhere on this path and there must not be one, for the reason the `MARGUS_PERSONA` bullet gives: a stance baked into a formula reaches every reader, including one whose whole portfolio is an index fund.
- **A fund and a coin get no valuation at all, and the guard is not tidiness.** Every method here prices a claim on a company's earnings. A fund owns hundreds of companies and its price is the sum of theirs by construction, so "priced like the average company" applied to an index fund is circular: measured on SPY it produced $619 against a price of $770, which reads as a judgement about the S&P 500 and is really the feed's own blended multiple divided by twenty. A coin has no earnings for any of it to divide. **Both are answered honestly elsewhere instead**, and that is where the interesting work is. A fund gets what it costs (the one number its holder controls, so it leads the figures), what is actually inside it, how concentrated that is, and **how much of it the reader already owns directly** (`fund-overlap.ts`), which is the exact answer to "am I spreading out or doubling down" and is invisible on every fund page in existence because it needs both halves. A coin gets its price and its range under a panel saying plainly that it files no accounts, earns nothing and owns nothing, so there is nothing to value it against except what somebody else will pay.
- **The top of the page answers the question this app may not answer, by answering a better one.** Somebody deciding wants to know whether the price is sensible, and Upside Lab is not an adviser: it may not say buy or sell, and a page that did would be wrong as well as illegal, because the answer depends on a holding period and a portfolio it cannot see. So `valueGlance` states, as a fact, **where today's price sits among the estimates just shown**, and hands back the question that decides it. "Above every estimate on this page, the highest of which is $618" is checkable against the methods listed below and tells the reader exactly what they would be betting on; "buy" tells them nothing. `value-glance.test.ts` fails on an instruction word in any output, and it earned that: it caught "you already hold ... so buying it would add to positions you have" in the fund overlap line, which is descriptive and reads as permission to somebody looking for permission. The growth rule of thumb is capped at **30 times**, not 40: past that it stops describing anything a large company sustains, and on Nvidia growing 66% the cap was the only thing setting the number, putting it $291 above the analysts' average. The analysts' consensus outweighs it, because forty-nine published targets are forty-nine people's work on the company and the multiple is one line of arithmetic on a heuristic. **"Inside the range" is only said when the range means something**: measured on AMD the methods ran from $151 to $618, and almost any price is inside a range that wide, so past a factor of 2.5 the copy says the methods disagree by that factor and that being inside it settles nothing. Beside it sit the three things a professional reads before deciding whether the rest is worth their time, each one figure and one line: what the price is assuming, **how much the analysts disagree** (every site prints the average target and hardly any print the range, which is the part that says whether the average is a consensus or the midpoint of an argument), and whether the profit is actually arriving, as last year's, this year's and next year's earnings per share in a row.
- **The one part of "should I buy this" this app will answer is the part that is arithmetic.** `position-fit.ts` is pure and has no model in it: put $2,000 into this and it becomes 7% of everything you own, your biggest group goes from 34% to 41%, and a quarter off it costs $600, which is 2% of the portfolio. Nobody can tell somebody whether a company is good; anybody can tell them that, and almost nowhere does. `concentrationNote` is the single shaped observation it is allowed to make and it is phrased with the figure in it rather than as advice. **Two failures here are worse than not shipping the card.** A browser that has not loaded the portfolio is not an empty portfolio: every share then works out against a total of zero and the card announces that this company would be a hundred per cent of everything the reader owns, which is a confident false statement about their own money, so `hasBook` tells the two apart and the card is simply absent for the second. And the money on it is **the reader's own currency, never the listing's**: a company quoted in euros inside a portfolio kept in dollars would otherwise have every figure labelled a hundred per cent wrong. Borrowed money counts against the portfolio the way it does everywhere else in this app, so a share of a portfolio carrying a loan is a bigger share.
- **A company page is written once and read by everybody, under the forecast cache's three rules plus one.** `portfell_company_briefs` is safe to share because nothing a reader types reaches the prompt: it is built from the provider's figures and public headlines, which is why it needs none of the machinery `runIsShareable` exists for. It ages out at five days (shorter than the forecast's fortnight, because a page carrying headlines and a case against goes stale in a way a five-year path does not), it is tied to the price it was written against, and `generated_at` is when the model wrote it and is never re-stamped on reuse, or a popular company becomes immortal and the age bound can never fire. The rule that is new is `facts_key`, a digest of the revenue, profit, debt and cash the page reasoned from, so the morning a company reports every reader gets a page written from the new numbers rather than a confident argument about figures that have been superseded. The five-year path uses the same prompt constant, the same years and the same shaping as the Growth room and is written into `portfell_forecast_ticker_cache`, so the two rooms cannot tell a reader two different stories about one company, and there is no floor, no lift and no minimum multiple anywhere on this path. **The figures and the prose are fetched apart and neither may become the other**: a page whose written half failed still arrives with everything a reader can check, and a company the feed barely covers gets its figures and no written page at all, because asking a model to write a case for and against from two numbers produces exactly the confident unfalsifiable paragraph the room was built to replace.
- **A price chart with no axis is a shape with no units, and the fix was to share the one that already had them.** The company room drew its five-year path as a bare sparkline, which reads well and tells a reader nothing they did not already know: they can see the line goes up and cannot see from what to what, which on a five-year price path is the only thing they wanted. `SheetPathChart` moved out of `ForecastPanel` into `src/components/ui/PathChart.tsx` unchanged, ticks, year rail and drag readout included, so both rooms draw one company's path the same way and cannot drift into telling the same story twice. The same applies to the finances: four years of revenue and profit were two bars a year with no scale, where a healthy company's profit bar is a tenth the height of its revenue bar and the thing you most wanted to read was a sliver. It is a table now, every figure printed with the margin worked out, and a bar behind the revenue column carrying the shape. Read the margin column downwards and you can see whether a business getting bigger is getting better at it, which the bars could never say.
- **`Scoreboard`'s column count is arithmetic, so the number of cells is a layout decision.** It refuses to leave an empty cell in the last row (`filledCardColumns`), which means it silently drops to whatever divides the children: nine readings at `cols={2}` became **one** column, and a page that should have been three tidy rows ran to a single tall stack of full-width cards on a 1280px screen, 13,996px long. At `cols={3}` the same page is 6,170px. A company gets nine readings, a fund four and a coin two, which fill exactly three rows, two and one. Changing how many figures a surface shows changes its layout, so count them against the column widths rather than adding one and hoping.
- **A page of identical rectangles is not a design, and the fix is a shape per question rather than more prose.** The first drafts of this room were nine equal cards, then a paragraph, then nine more, and the complaint it drew was that it had no character and was very wordy without substance. Every answer here is structural rather than decorative. **Three readings lead and the rest are reference**: `LEAD_READINGS` (what it costs against its earnings, whether those earnings are growing, whether the business keeps what it sells) take an accent rule along the top and keep their what-ordinary-looks-like line, and the other six shrink one step on the type ladder and move that line onto the label's own tip, because nine cards each carrying a figure and two sentences is twenty-seven lines of prose in one panel and reads as an essay with numbers in it. **The hierarchy is made by shrinking the reference cells, never by growing the leads**: a scoreboard figure is already at the top of the ladder (`text-2xl`), and the invariant that keeps UI type on the ladder fails on the `text-3xl` that reaching the other way needs. **The second line of every cell starts at the same height**, which needs a `min-h` on the figure row rather than `Score`'s own bottom alignment, because a benchmark chip is taller than the digits beside it and a grid whose sentences all begin somewhere else is what reads as thrown together. **Colour carries meaning and nothing else**: a beat is a `border-l-gain` card and a miss `border-l-loss`, a margin bar is `bg-gain` unless the margin is negative, and no panel is tinted.
- **Four quarters, the results days, and where each $100 goes, because a year is too coarse to see a business turning.** `CompanyFinancials` is three blocks and each answers a question the annual table cannot. `QuarterTable` is the last four quarters with the margin worked out, which is where Meta's 5.3% quarter sits next to its 47.5% one; `Surprises` is the four results days against what analysts expected, printed as beat or missed with both figures and the date, because a company whose forecasts keep being wrong is a company whose forecasts should be trusted less and that fact is on no other page in this app; and `QualityLadder` is gross, operating and profit margin as three bars on one scale, so the gap between the top bar and the bottom is everything the company spends between selling something and keeping the money. **Return on equity is deliberately not one of those bars**, because it is not a share of revenue and putting it on the same scale would be a category error drawn as a fact. **Both financial tables carry a bar behind the revenue figure and a second small bar in the margin column, each scaled to its own column**: a profit margin drawn on the revenue scale is a sliver, which is exactly what made the chart these tables replaced useless.
- **Valuation is one panel, every method is in it, and the arithmetic sits behind the mark rather than under it.** The figure and the working were two panels stacked on each other, `ValueGlance` and `FairValueCard`, so a reader met two headings, two subtitles and two rounds of scaffolding for one question. They are one panel read top to bottom: where the price sits, what that means, then every method. **A method is never hidden**: its name, its price, its weight, its distance from today and the assumption it rests on are all on the page, and the assumption is the part a reader argues with. What moved is `working`, the line naming the inputs, into an `InfoTip` on the method's own name, because three of those stacked under three assumptions is six paragraphs in a panel somebody opened to see one number. That distinction is the rule: **the claim and its premise stay visible, the arithmetic may be one press away.** Each method draws a `GapBar`, a bar growing left or right of today's price, capped at 60% so one wild method cannot squash the others to nothing. The row's metadata is sentence case, not the label tier: uppercase mono is the voice of a short fixed label and a whole line of varying content set in it shouts. And the confidence line prints only when it says something the cards do not, which is one method or none; a reader can count three.
- **The top of the page is a line with two marks on it, and everything around it is one figure or one sentence.** `ValueGlance` was three tall cards each carrying a figure and a paragraph, a hairline nobody could see, and then three more cards: about 900px to say one thing. The whole content of the panel is where one price sits against a band of estimates, which is a picture, so the picture is the hero and it measures **380px** now. Four rules came out of shrinking it. **A label that names a mark goes above that mark, never at the end of the row**: the price was a 3px hairline with its figure printed at the far edge, so the one thing the panel exists for was the least visible thing on it and a reader had to measure across the panel to pair a mark with its number; both marks now carry their own name and figure directly overhead, and the price is a ringed gold pill. **The label's anchor is clamped and the mark's is not**, or a price at either end of the band would be drawn in the wrong place to make a caption fit, and two labels closer than `LABEL_GAP` are pushed apart symmetrically, which is not a rare case since it fires exactly when the price and the estimate agree. **A cell may not print a figure the picture directly above it just drew**: the strip held the share price and the estimate, which are the two marks, so a third of the panel was repeating what the reader had been shown; it carries the gap, the growth the price needs, the year the shares have had and the earnings line every method is a multiple of, none of which appear anywhere above. **A missing reading drops its cell rather than filling it with `n/a`**, and the column count follows the cell count, for the same reason `filledCardColumns` exists. Two cards went entirely: the **analyst target range**, whose spread is a fact about analysts rather than about the company and which already has a line inside the method that uses it, and **earnings per share**, which is the input to every method below rather than a reading of its own.
- **A panel whose sections are prose sets its own vertical rhythm, and it needs one child to do it.** `Panel` spaces its direct children at `gap-5 sm:gap-6`, which is right for a panel of short blocks and far too tight for the company room's two big ones: four sections of bars, cards and paragraphs stacked at 24px read as one wall, and the complaint they drew was claustrophobic rather than cluttered. A direct child may not add its own `mt-*` (the panel rule above says why: it gets both), so `ValueGlance` and `BusinessPanel` wrap their bodies in a single child at `gap-10 sm:gap-12` and the decision lives with the content. Inside a section the scale steps down rather than off: `gap-8` between bars, `gap-5` between method cards, `gap-3` and `p-5` inside one. **The instinct to reach for when a surface feels crowded is more space, not less content**; three rounds of this room were spent deleting things that were not the problem.
- **A figure pinned to the panel's edge is a figure with nothing beside it.** The period bars printed the revenue right-aligned on its own row, so it landed at the far right of the panel while the grey bar it named ended somewhere in the middle: a reader saw a bar stop, and a number a hand's width away, with nothing connecting them, and reasonably asked what the grey meant. The revenue is the bar's own length, so it now sits on the label line with the period, and the bar below is simply that figure drawn; what stays under the bar is the one number the bar splits out, left-aligned under the green it names. Nothing in these rows is right-aligned. The legend moved to the **top** of the list for the same reason: a key a reader only meets after scrolling past everything it explains has explained nothing.
- **The picture carries both marks, every method is a tick on it, and the labels hang from a band of their own.** The blended estimate was pulled off the track and printed as a row underneath, on the argument that the picture answers one question and a second mark competes with it for the same forty pixels. Read for real that cost the one comparison the panel exists to draw: where the estimate stands against today is a distance along a line, and turning it back into two numbers in two places asks the reader to do in their head what the picture was already doing. It is back, the label over it says **In 12 months** rather than "Estimate" (a word stamped over a figure whose horizon was already underneath it), and nothing prints the same figure twice, so the row below is gone. **Three faults were found by rendering the real component and measuring it, after two rounds of reasoning about it got them wrong.** (1) **A label that hangs off the track grows upward, so its clearance depends on how many lines it happens to have.** With the gap note under the price, the estimate mark's first line came within **12px of the subtitle on a laptop and 8px on a phone**, and the two marks' names sat 16px apart because one was two lines and the other three. No amount of `mt-*` fixes that. The labels have a fixed `h-[3.5rem]` band and hang from its top: every name on one row, every price on the row under it, a note on a third where there is one, and the clearance above set by this file rather than left over. (2) **A bar cannot be captioned into meaning.** It was a grey bar on a slightly darker grey bar and the reader could see one was brighter and had no way to learn what either meant; the figures naming the band's ends were printed at the panel's edges and the caption under the panel's middle. Naming the parts in place was necessary and not sufficient, and the thing that actually explains it is drawing **each surviving method as its own tick**: three ticks and a grey span behind them is self-evidently the range of three estimates, the bright mark among them is their blend, and the caption counts what the reader can see ("all 3 methods below") instead of asserting something ("every estimate") they had to take on trust. The scale is a hairline so a thin rule reads as the ruler and a thick bar as the reading. (3) **A band with no width is not a band.** One surviving method makes the low and the high the same number: the span drew as zero-width and the collision guard pushed the two end figures apart into `$120.00` printed twice, which reads as a broken page rather than as a company only one method could be run on. `hasBand` withholds the span, the ticks and both end figures; the mark and its caption are the whole honest picture there. **A label's width is what decides whether two marks can stand near each other**, so the gap note is the bare percentage: "+16.6% against today" was three times wider than the price above it and, on a 342px track with the estimate a few per cent from the price, printed straight through the gold mark's own figure. `spread` pushes colliding anchors apart symmetrically and the marks never move. The lesson under all three: **render the component and measure it.** Every one of these was invisible to reasoning about the markup and obvious in one screenshot, and `value-glance.test.ts` now holds each rule.
- **One legal line for the product, not a hedge on every panel.** The valuation subtitle ended "Upside Lab is not an adviser and will not tell you whether to buy it" and the methods ended "None of it is a price target or anybody telling you to buy or sell". `ADVICE_DISCLAIMER_SHORT` is the legal line and it is said once, which is what the disclaimer bullet above already required; a second and third hedge bolted onto the panels a reader came for reads as nervousness and teaches them to skip the sentence that matters. What keeps the room safe is structural instead: no output on this path contains a verdict word, `valueGlance` states where the price sits rather than what to do, and `value-glance.test.ts` fails on an instruction word.
- **A figure with nothing to compare it to is not information, and three of them in a row is worse.** The strip under the picture carried an earnings-per-share ramp, `$26.83 -> $31.37 -> $33.95`, three rising numbers with nothing saying what they were rising from, towards, or over what period. It is gone: every method's working line already states the earnings it multiplied with the year attached, which is the same figure with the context that makes it mean something. The rule generalises past this cell. A number belongs where its unit of comparison already is.
- **A sentence about the page must name the source, not the page.** `valueGlance` said "the lowest estimate on this page", which points at the paper it is printed on; it says "The lowest is $678.92, from the growth multiple" now, reading the `source` field every `FairValueMethod` carries (the analysts' average, the growth multiple, the model's own path). A reader deciding whether to argue with a figure needs to know whose it is, which is the whole design of this room.
- **The years and the quarters are one panel, because they were one subject at two time scales.** There was a year table inside Key financials and a separate "The last four quarters" panel under it, each with its own heading, its own subtitle and its own copy of revenue, profit and margin, so a reader had to decide which of the two to read. `BusinessPanel` is both behind one `Segmented`: pick the scale and everything answers at that scale. `CompanyFinancials.tsx` and `YearTable` are gone. **A period is one quantity split in two, so it is one bar rather than a row of columns.** It was tried as two unscaled bars (a chart with no axis, and a healthy company's profit bar is a tenth its revenue bar, so the figure you most wanted was a sliver), then as four printed columns with a bar behind one of them, which is accurate, complete and still a spreadsheet where nothing says which of the four numbers is the story. Each period is now **one bar, its length the revenue against the biggest period shown, with the profit filled inside it**, and **both widths share that one scale**, so the filled fraction of a bar is that period's margin and the filled parts compare across periods as well as within one. Drawing a margin on its own scale would let a small period's profit look bigger than a large one's, which is the lie the two-bar version told. **The figures go under the bar at every width, and the bar is pure shape.** Printing them inside it reads beautifully on a laptop and broke on a phone, which is what shipped: at 390px the profit and revenue figures were drawn over each other and a quarter read `$2.7 billion ke$51.2 billion`. No threshold fixes it, because how much text a block can hold depends on the device rather than on the company, so the same period reads one way on a laptop and another on a phone. The general rule: **a label placed inside a data mark is sized by the viewport, never by the data, so it belongs beside the mark unless the mark has a fixed pixel width.**
- **The research room was rendered with the app's own CSS and measured at four widths, and five faults fell out that no amount of reading the markup had found.** The harness is worth rebuilding rather than the fixes worth memorising: render the real components to static markup, compile `globals.css` through the project's own Tailwind, screenshot at 1100, 820, 390 and 360, and then **compare every text node's `getClientRects()` against every other's** rather than eyeballing. (Use the per-line rects, not `getBoundingClientRect`: an inline element that wraps returns a union box covering both lines and every wrap reads as a collision.) What it found. **A label and the figure under it may not disagree**: the 52 week range cell printed "52 week range" over `44%`, which is where today's price sits *inside* the range and is not a range, so read label-then-figure like every other cell it stated something false. The label is the ordinary financial name and stays, so the figure is now `$480 to $791` and the position moved to the chip; cents are dropped above $100 and kept below it, where "$5 to $9" would be a different range from the real one. **A separator may not be the last thing on a line**: the valuation method's metadata row wraps, and at 390px it wrapped as "Arithmetic on the accounts ·" with the dot stranded, so the dots are `hidden sm:inline` and below that width the gap does the separating. **A grid stretches its children**, so with three points for and two against the case-against card measured 399px tall with its content ending at 264 -- a third of a bordered card blank, which reads as content that failed to arrive rather than as a shorter answer; `items-start`. **An odd number of source cards left the last one alone in a row with a hole beside it**, and there are three to six of them depending on the company, so the last takes the whole row when it would otherwise be orphaned. **One list, one date format**: `formatRelativeTime` turns absolute after a week, so the articles read "3d ago" over "Aug 28" over "Aug 21" and could not be ordered by eye, which is the one thing a date in that list is for. `stock-room.test.ts` and `company-facts.test.ts` hold all five.
- **A cache that holds a whole record carries the record's shape in its key, and forgetting that crashed a room.** `fetchCompanyFactsCached` (`src/lib/market/fundamentals.ts`) caches a whole `CompanyFacts` under `unstable_cache`. `quarters`, `surprises`, `operatingMargin` and `returnOnEquity` were added to the type while the key stayed `company-facts-v1`, so for an hour after that deploy every reader got an entry written by the old code, `facts.quarters.length` threw on an absent array, and the quarterly panel showed its error card. **It heals itself when the entry expires, which is what made it look intermittent and is not a reason to leave it**: bump the version in the same commit that adds or removes a field. The reader half is a floor for when somebody forgets: `CompanyFinancials` reads `facts.quarters ?? []`, because a bare `.length` on a field that a cache from a previous deploy does not carry is a crash rather than an empty panel.
- **The analysts are weighted by how much they agree with each other, never by who they are.** The obvious ask is to weight them individually: more for the ones with a good record or a view the house shares, less for the ones who are structurally cautious. Two reasons it is not done and must not be. The feed publishes an average, a high, a low and a count and **no names at all**, so there is nothing to weight one by; and leaning towards the analysts who agree with a house view is that house view wearing somebody else's research as a costume, which reaches every reader of the app including one who would take the other side, and is the same thing `MARGUS_PERSONA` had removed from it. What is measurable, and is the question underneath, is whether these people agree with **each other**: forty targets clustered inside twenty per cent is a consensus whose average means something, and forty running from half the price to double it is a contested name where the average is the midpoint of an argument and describes nobody's actual view. So `consensusMethod` multiplies its coverage weight by an agreement factor read off `analystSpread`, and says so in its own `assumes` line when it has discounted itself. It cuts the weight in both directions at once, which is what keeps it a measurement rather than an opinion.
- **A column of yearly totals says almost nothing, so the year table carries the growth between them.** Revenue rising every year is what almost every company's table looks like; what decides whether a business is winning or running out of road is whether each year's rise is bigger or smaller than the last, and that is invisible in a column of totals. `YearTable` computes it from the row above rather than taking it from the feed, so the first year has none and says `NO_VALUE`. It is hidden below `sm` along with the bars, because five columns do not fit a phone and the margin is the one a beginner can act on.
- **Research is published, and the whole design of that is a bound on what a stranger can make this app spend.** `/research/<ticker>` is the same room a signed-in reader gets with the reader's own half taken out, and it is public, indexed, in the sitemap and linked from the landing footer. It exists because the queries people actually type are "AAPL fair value" and "TSLA price target", and this app already answers those better than the pages that currently rank: every figure named, every method's assumption printed, and a link back to the source. **What is subtracted is everything that needs a portfolio behind it**: what you own of it, what buying it would do to your mix, and your own price plan. That is not tidiness, it is the `hasBook` rule again, since a browser with no portfolio in it is not an empty portfolio and a card that treats the two the same makes a confident false statement about somebody's money. **A page view may never call a model, and that is enforced by the shape of the code rather than by care.** `buildCompanyPage` (`src/lib/company/page-build.ts`) is the one builder, shared by the app's route, the public page and the cron, and it takes a `generate` boolean: the app's route passes true, because a person with an account is waiting, and `page-data.ts` passes false and takes no option that could change it. If a page view could generate, one crawler walking the sitemap would spend a run per company in about two minutes, with a search engine holding the trigger. The cold page then shows the figures and no argument, which is the state the room already has for a failed run, and `/api/cron/research-briefs` writes the argument in behind it, oldest brief first, **eight companies a run and four runs a day**, so the ceiling on the whole section is 32 model calls a day and the list is walked in three days against the store's own five day expiry. `research-seo.test.ts` fails if the list outgrows the schedule. **The universe is a closed list** (`src/lib/research/universe.ts`) checked before a single fetch, which is what makes the crawl surface, the provider spend and the sitemap the same number by construction; a symbol outside it is a page that does not exist rather than one that failed, and it 404s. It is a list of listings and not of opinions, on the same argument that removed the per-ticker baselines: nothing is on it because anybody believes in it. The groups are the only internal linking the section has, and `researchNeighbours` walks from each ticker's own position so a group's members do not all point at the same six.
- **The caching is three layers and the numbers were measured off the real build, not assumed.** The page is `export const revalidate = 21600` with `dynamicParams` on and `generateStaticParams` returning **nothing**: pre-rendering a hundred pages at build would put a hundred provider calls into every deploy on a free tier, with a provider having a bad minute failing somebody else's unrelated deploy, and worse, a transient failure would bake a 404 for that company for six hours. - **The marker stretches, and that is the whole of why it reads as iOS.** A single neutral pill (`bg-foreground/10`) moves behind the cells rather than a fill appearing on one and disappearing from another, which is what makes a row of glyphs read as one place with a marker in it. What was added on 2026-08-30 is that it no longer arrives as the rectangle it left as: its **leading edge sets off before its trailing edge follows**, so it smears across the ground it covers and gathers itself back up. That means the marker is **two insets, `left` and `right`, each with its own duration** (260ms leading, 460ms trailing, `src/app/dock.css`), never a position and a width. The stretch is the transition rather than a keyframe laid over it, so it **scales with the distance**: measured on a 120px cell, one cell across peaks at 163px (1.36x), five cells across at 326px (2.72x), and both settle at exactly 120. The trailing curve overshoots by a hair, so the pill pinches to 118px on arrival before resting, which is the 2px that reads as liquid. **Edges, never a transform**: a transform cannot stretch a pill without stretching its round ends into ellipses, and both panes are absolutely positioned so nothing else is laid out. The cost that came with it is real and is guarded: a pane's width now changes on every frame of every travel, so the resize observer watches `[data-dock-cell]` rather than every child, or a measurement and a layout read run on each of those frames to answer a question about cells that have not moved. **No `backdrop-filter` on either pane**, deliberately -- iOS refracts what the pill passes over, and the web translation is a filtered element moving over content inside an already-filtered fixed bar, which is the landing page's 42-repainted-frames fault, plus a blurred label on the room you are navigating to. - **The two docks no longer breathe at the same moment, because they do not have the same input.** The phone bar is six glyphs, scaling it is free at every CPU throttle, and a finger has no hover to spend motion on, so **all of its motion is in the travel** and it takes the fuller breath: `DOCK_MOTION.phone` is 3% over 460ms with a 340ms travel and an 18ms lag, near the reference's own 4% over 500ms rather than half of it. Measured on the real bar: **+3.00% on both axes**, peak at 234ms (40% of its life, as traced), a -0.10% undershoot, settled by 484ms, and a one-cell move stretching the pill 1.22x over 21 painted frames with an 8.6px worst step. **The laptop bar answers a click instantly and breathes only on the pointer.** `travelMs: 0`, so `glide` writes the resting geometry and returns: the marker is simply *there* on the cell you pressed, on `pointerdown`, rather than sliding across the bar to agree with you. The travelling pill is the piece of this design that reads worst with a pointer -- a finger has nothing else going on while it waits, so a marker crossing the bar is company, but a mouse has already arrived and watching the bar take 220ms to catch up is the bar being slower than you are. The phone keeps its travel; different input, different numbers. The swell is off the click too (`swellPeak: 1`, which `swellFrames` answers with null rather than sixteen frames of `scale(1)`). Two reasons pointing the same way. A capsule that lurches every time you press it is arguing with a decision already made: you know you pressed, and the marker is already saying where you are going. And **scaling nine cells of 14px type re-rasterises them, which Chrome will not composite** -- neither `will-change` nor dropping the backdrop filter recovers it -- so the loudest motion in the bar was also the only one being paid for on every navigation. What replaced it is the gesture the Margus button makes (`hover:scale-[1.015]`): `.dock-breathe` in `dock.css`, a **transition and not an animation**, because this is a state the bar sits in for as long as it is being pointed at. It is **0.6%** and deliberately barely there -- about 3.5px an edge on a 1,164px bar, enough to say the bar noticed the pointer and no more. (It was 1.5%, measured uniform to 0.0000 points and back to exactly rest on leave, and read as too much.) **0.000px of swing across a whole press**, which is the point of it. `hoverPeak`/`hoverMs` live in `DOCK_MOTION.wide` and reach the CSS as custom properties, so every number the dock moves by is still in one file. Gated on `(hover: hover) and (pointer: fine)` or a touch screen latches `:hover` after a tap and leaves the bar permanently 1.5% larger; there is deliberately **no `:active` rule**, since the cells have their own press and the capsule holding still under the finger is what makes that press read as landing on something solid. **Frame cost, measured over eight cycles of each:** at 1x, **zero long frames on all three** (hover swell 0/313, marker travel 0/182, phone travel 0/254, worst 16.8ms everywhere). The hover swell's own share, on against off, is 1 frame at 4x and 7 at 6x; desktops are 1x-2x machines. **Do not "simplify" this by scaling only the glass and holding the words still**: the labels still move and the bar becomes two objects rather than one. **The rim carries the glass, not the body.** `.glass-dock::after` adds three inset shadows -- a brighter near face along the top, a dark line just inside it for the thickness of the pane, and a soft bounce off the inner bottom wall. It paints once with the capsule and never animates, so it cannot drop a frame, and it is on a pseudo-element because `.card-sheen.glass` already owns that element's `box-shadow` and restating its list to append is how the two drift apart. **It is deliberately not refraction**: real liquid glass bends its backdrop, which on the web needs `backdrop-filter: url(#…)` with a displacement map -- verified to work in Chromium and not supported by WebKit, so on the iPhone this app is judged by it would do nothing, and it is a filter recomputed every frame on a fixed element, which is the landing page's own repaint fault.
- **The marker runs on the compositor, and that is what a route change was breaking.** It used to be a CSS transition on `left` and `right`, which reads well and cannot survive a navigation: those are layout properties, so every frame is laid out and painted on the main thread, and the main thread is exactly what a route change is busy with. **Measured off a recording of the real app on a phone**, tracking the pill's centre frame by frame, every travel ran two or three frames, stalled four to six while the new room rendered, then teleported the rest in one frame (+77px, +106px, +75px). Reproduced in a harness with a 180ms main-thread block at 4x CPU and read off **painted** frames (a main-thread probe cannot see this, by construction, so it is a CDP screencast with the marker recoloured to make it findable): the old build stalled mid-travel for 1, 5, 1 and 2 frames and then jumped **166px in a single frame**; the new one steps 3, 7, 10, 16, 27, 39, 34, 22, 14, 10, 7, 6, 4, 2 with one 1-frame stall, which is an ease curve rather than a freeze. `transform` is the only way out, and what it cannot do is ease two edges independently the way two transitions could, so `travelKeyframes` samples the two eased edges at **8ms** and hands them over as keyframes -- that sampling is what "more frames" means, since the browser then has a value for every frame it could draw. **The pill's width is set to its destination before the animation starts**, so `scaleX` is exactly 1 at rest and the round caps are true circles whenever it is still; they go slightly oval only in flight, which is why `MARKER_LAG_MS` is small (12ms, a one-cell move peaking at 1.16x rather than the reference's 1.29x) -- a big lag on a 48px circle reads as an egg. Never put dock geometry back on `left`/`right`, and never let the resting `scaleX` be anything but 1.
- **The capsule breathes, and the WHOLE bar breathes as one object. This is the phone bar's motion now, and only the phone bar's** (the laptop breathes on the pointer instead; see the bullet above). It is a uniform scale, and a one-axis scale is the thing to never do.** Traced frame by frame off the reference recording at 30fps, fitting the capsule's own four edges sub-pixel: width and height move together and by the same fraction (n53 +1.96%/+2.04%, n56 **+3.99%/+3.95%**, n58 +2.66%/+2.31%, n60 +0.67%/+0.68%), symmetric about the centre on both axes -- at the peak the left edge moved -23.8px against the right's +23.7px and the top -3.8px against the bottom +3.9px. So `transform: scale()` on the capsule itself, contents included, no `transform-origin`, no lean. **Two earlier readings of this were wrong and both are worth knowing.** A vertical measuring window that missed the capsule's real edges reported the height as a constant 234px when the true height is 187px, and that one number produced a `scaleX`; scaling on one axis stretches letterforms sideways, which is what makes the bar feel wrong, where a uniform scale magnifies type instead of distorting it. Then, over-correcting, the glass was moved to its own layer so only the material scaled -- also wrong, because the reference plainly moves everything together and a bar whose rim slides while its words stand still is two objects, not one. **The shape is a swell, not a snap**: normalised against its own peak it runs 0, 0.26, 0.35, 0.49, 0.74, 0.94, **1.00 at 40% of its life**, 0.90, 0.67, 0.41, 0.17, 0.05, then **under rest to -0.035** before settling, over 500ms. The undershoot is the point; an ease with the peak at 11% and no undershoot is a flinch. Samples are one recorded frame apart so they are interpolated linearly, because the curve is carried by the data and an easing on top would be a guess about a measured shape. **The marker's two edges take the same duration and the same curve, and the trailing one is simply delayed** (350ms, `cubic-bezier(0.5, 0.2, 0.05, 0.95)` fitted numerically to the reference's own pill to sse 0.0011, 28ms lag): a constant lag is the back of a blob following the front at a fixed distance, which is why the reference reads as one object, where two different durations read as a rectangle being stretched. Measured against the recording, a one-cell move peaks at **1.28x at t=133ms** against the reference's **1.29x at t=133ms**. A six-cell walk reaches 2.5x, which is the same physics at six times the velocity and is left alone. **The cost is real and lands in one place**: scaling text re-rasterises it, so the labelled laptop bar is free at 1x and 4x CPU throttling (0 and 1 long frames) and costs at 6x (30 against 4) and 10x (78 of 150 against 19); `will-change: transform` does not recover it. The glyph-only phone bar is free at every throttle. Desktops are 1x-2x machines, so this was the right trade while the laptop bar paid it at all; it does not any more, and the shape below now runs on the glyph-only bar it was traced from.
- **The marker leaves on the press, and the route only confirms it.** `activeId` is read from `usePathname()` (`Dashboard`), so every bit of the dock's motion used to be downstream of the App Router committing: the bar was tied to the network rather than to the finger, and prefetching only makes that wait short rather than making it not a wait. iOS moves its indicator on touch-down, and `useDockMarker` does now: a `pointerdown` on a cell sets `aimed`, `measure` positions the marker at `aimed.current ?? on`, and the router arriving on that same cell simply clears the bet with the marker already there. **It is a bet, so it has to be able to lose, and it loses three ways**: the release landed somewhere other than the cell it started on (a press dragged off is not a tap), the room answered with a different cell or the cell stopped existing, or nothing answered at all within `AIM_GIVES_UP_MS`. That last number is **4000ms and is deliberately long** -- snapping the marker home mid-wait looks far more broken than letting it stand where the reader put it. Only a plain primary press bets: a middle click or a held modifier opens the room in **another tab**, and a marker moving for a room this tab is not in is the one way this is worse than waiting. Only `[data-dock-goes]` cells bet, so the add cell (a dialog) and the folded picker (a menu) never move it. Verified against a harness with a 600ms simulated router: the marker is already travelling 16ms after `pointerdown` and lands exactly on the cell; a press dragged off returns it; a refused navigation leaves it standing at 2.3s and returns it at 4s. **A bet the router is about to confirm is not called off, and that took a second pass to get right.** `callOff` repositions the marker to whatever is still lit, and during a navigation that is the cell you are *leaving*, so any pointer event landing off the cell while the room rendered sent the marker home and back. Measured off a recording of the real app: the marker completed its travel, sat on the new cell for about 350ms, then teleported back and replayed the whole journey the moment the room arrived. Reproduced against the real component with a 350ms commit, it logged two travels 7ms apart, `312 -> 4` then `4 -> 312`. So **a click on the aimed cell commits it** (`going`), after which only the room answering or the timeout settles the marker; a `pointercancel` *before* the click is still a genuinely abandoned press. And a bet that does lose **arrives rather than travels** (`reverting`), with no swell, because reverting is a correction and animating it draws a second full trip across the bar for a room nobody went to. `travelKeyframes` also runs inside `pointerdown`, before the browser can dispatch the click, so the travel curve is solved **once into a 1,024-point table** rather than binary-searched twice per sample -- about 2,100 solver iterations a tap for a curve that never changes. **AND A BAR IN A HIDDEN ROOM MUST NOT MEASURE ITSELF, WHICH IS THE ONE THAT PUT THE MARKER ON THE WRONG CELL.** `WorkspaceShell` keeps every visited room mounted behind `hidden` and each room draws its own dock, so there are two of these on the book and three once you have been to Circle. A hidden element has no layout box, so `offsetLeft` and `offsetWidth` are both **0**, and measuring one records `{left: 0, width: 0}` as the marker's last known place; show that room again and the travel is computed from there, which is a zero-width pill at the far left sweeping the whole bar. Worse, the cell you *press* belongs to the bar about to be hidden while the bar you end up looking at is a different element with a different hook instance, so the bet is placed on one bar and settled on another. `onScreen` gates it: no layout box, no measuring, no animating, no state written, and the outstanding bet dies with the room. The first measure after a room is shown again **arrives rather than travels**. **Measured by walking the real app** (demo mode, canonical seed, the real dock) through twelve room changes at 1x, 4x and 6x CPU: before the guard, **7 of 36 landed on the wrong cell and 105 painted frames carried a collapsed marker at the bar's left edge**; after it, **0 and 0**. This is the check to run on any change to this hook -- a unit test cannot see it, because it only happens when more than one dock is alive at once.
- **A bet cannot be settled by the thing the bet itself moves, and getting that wrong cost the reader four seconds of the wrong room.** `measure` used to end the press's bet on `on === aimed.current`: the router had answered when the lit cell was the pressed one. That was sound while `[data-on]` came from `pathname` alone, and it stopped being sound the moment the *page* began answering the press too (`route-aim.ts`, the bullet above): the aim reaches `Dashboard` in the same event, the room it names renders, and that render lights the pressed cell -- so the bet was declared won about two frames after it was placed, before the finger had come up. **Everything the bar does to stand down begins `if (!aimed.current) return`**, so all of it went at once: a release off the cell, a `pointercancel`, and the four-second backstop all became no-ops, `aimRoute(null)` was never published, and nothing ever told the page its bet had lost. On a phone a dock press very often does not become a navigation -- a touch that lands while the page is still flinging is spent stopping the fling -- so this was not rare. **Measured off a screen recording of the real app**: press Home while on `/lab`, and Home is on screen for **4.000s to the frame** and then Lab returns, with the address never having changed; reproduced against the real build at 390x844, 4000ms before, **140ms after**. The rule now is that the **router** settles it (`pathRef.current !== aimedFrom.current`, which is the room answering whether it answered with this cell or, on a redirect, another one), and the marker separately stops betting on a cell that has stopped existing. Two things fall out and both are load-bearing. `published` is tracked **apart from** `aimed`, because the two come apart: a press on Circle mounts Circle's room on the press, which hides the book and the bar the finger is on, while the page is still drawing the room that press asked for -- so `callOff` asks "was the page told", never "is the marker still betting". And the click gets a deadline of its own (`CLICK_FOLLOWS_MS`, 300ms): a press that leaves an ordinary `pointerup` on the cell and simply never fires a click is invisible to both the release rule and `pointercancel`, and it is the commonest of the three on a phone. The reverse trap is in the hidden-bar guard: that bar must keep its backstop armed (a cancelled cross-room press still has to put the reader back) but **must drop it once the address has moved**, or it fires four seconds later and withdraws somebody else's aim. The general lesson, which is the one to carry: **when a prediction starts driving the UI, every check written against that UI stops being evidence.**
- **A dock cell takes its own press, because the browser will not always turn one into a click, and a tab bar you have to press twice is not a tab bar.** The bullet above got the bar to admit a lost press promptly instead of after four seconds, and promptly admitting the tap did nothing is still the tap doing nothing. **A press becomes a navigation by becoming a click, and the browser decides whether to make one.** On a phone it often decides not to, for reasons that have nothing to do with what the reader meant: a touch landing while the page is still flinging is spent stopping the fling, and a drag begun on the bar pans the document, because a `fixed` element is still a pan target. Both take the press and leave no click behind, and both are commonest in exactly the moment this bar matters most, which is while the reader is still scrolling. A tab bar is not page content -- on iOS the native one is outside the scroll view and works while the list is still moving -- so `useDockMarker` judges the tap on its own evidence and calls `router.push` itself: landed on a cell, released on that cell or taken from it having never wandered past `TAP_SLOP` (10px, the platform's own figure, against a 48px cell), and not held past `TAP_HOLD_MS` (700ms), which is somebody asking for the browser's link preview rather than the room. The rail's price fields are `EoyPriceInput fill`, **bounded at `w-24`, never `w-full`**: the edit cue is a dashed `border-bottom` on the element itself, so a full-width field draws a rule clean across the row and reads as a separator, which `globals.css` warns about in as many words. Height sits on the row (`min-h-12`), not the input, because an input given a 44px min-height centres its digits and leaves the dash floating ten pixels below them. `src/lib/forecast-years.test.ts` fails on a hardcoded forecast year or on a second, narrower list of years anywhere in the panel: the years are a range that moves, and a layout that only fits four of them is a layout problem, never a reason to show three quarters of a forecast.
- **The retirement module answers "when can I stop", and almost every decision in it is a refusal to make the flattering simplification.** It is Growth's second sub-tab (`GrowthRoom`, `?growthtab=retirement`) rather than a room, because a dock cell exists for a reader's data and never for a route, and a seventh glyph on a 374px phone bar costs every other cell width for a page opened a handful of times a year. All the arithmetic is pure and tested in `src/lib/retirement/`; the panels only draw. **Everything is in today's money**, because returns are real returns, which is why there is no inflation field: offering one invites counting it twice, the commonest way a page like this is wrong by a factor rather than a margin. The plan never leaves the browser (`state.ts`, `localStorage`), so nothing asks for a salary. Six things earned their place the hard way.
  **A life expectancy is the wrong statistic three times over and all three errors push the same way.** The figure in the news is from birth and a reader of this page has already not died young; a published table is a *period* table that assumes medicine stops today; and half of people outlive an average by definition, while the two errors cost wildly different amounts, a surplus against destitution at 92. So `longevity.ts` is Gompertz and Makeham with the slope fixed (the striking finding about these curves is how little the *rate* of ageing varies between populations) and the **level solved from the one longevity figure every country publishes and anybody can look up, further years at 65**, which is why it is a fit rather than a baked table nobody could check. The default plans to the one in twenty age, not the average. **The old age taper is load bearing and was found by reading the output**: a flat 1% a year compounded over the 75 years to 106 halves mortality at every age and put a one in twenty chance on reaching 105, which no projection supports; faded out between 90 and 110 it lands on 103, and the one in ten age comes out at 100.3, which is the published cohort figure. Verify a change here against that number.
  **Every year of retirement is costed on its own, never averaged.** A pension starting at 67, a mortgage ending in year six and a child turning 18 each change one year, and the years that break a retirement are the early ones, so an average gets the total roughly right and the shape wrong. **Tax is grossed up rather than taken off**: the published baskets are after tax figures, so landing 30,000 through a 15% charge costs 30,000/0.85, and subtracting instead leaves the plan about a year of spending short. **The lifelong figure is the plan's LAST year, not its smallest**: by then everything temporary has ended and every pension has started, whereas the minimum could be one cheap year in the middle mistaken for the rest of a life.
  **Two answers are printed and the gap between them is the product.** The exact present value of every year's shortfall, and the lifelong spending funded at a safe withdrawal rate with everything temporary funded from capital on top. That split is not decoration: a debt with an end date and a lifetime of groceries are different financial objects. The rate starts at the published ladder (Bengen 1994, Trinity 1998: 4% over 30 years, US shares and bonds, before fees) and has two corrections shown separately and both switchable, half a point for using the world's markets rather than the luckiest one and the reader's own fees. Under about ten years it stops being a safe rate at all and becomes arithmetic, which is what `fixedHorizonPot` is for.
  **`earliestRetirement` is a fixed point and watching a pot cross a line is the bug it exists to prevent.** Stopping five years sooner adds five years of spending, removes five of saving and lengthens the horizon, which lowers the safe rate and raises the pot: the target climbs to meet the pot. Measured on the canonical case the naive threshold said 49 and the fixed point says 51, and the error is always in the flattering direction.
  **Three faults only a screenshot found, which is this file's standing rule again.** `NativeSelect` IS the select, and nesting one inside it rendered the country picker empty. Two mark captions under the survival chart printed through each other into "1 in 10 1P2an 105" whenever the reader's planning age sat near a percentile, which is not a rare case but the ordinary one; the labels are bare ages spread apart symmetrically now, with the names in a legend that cannot collide, and the marks themselves never move. And a band of the glide path drawn as one wrapping flex row collapsed at 390px into nine unrelated controls with no way to tell which age went with which share.
  **A SAFE WITHDRAWAL RATE DOES NOT APPLY TO A POT THAT EARNS A CERTAIN NOTHING, and getting that wrong made the grid contradict its own caption.** The cash column ran the same machinery as the invested one with the returns zeroed, so it divided by a rate the literature derived from the worst ORDER of equity returns. Cash has no order to get wrong: at zero real return a 43 year retirement supports exactly 1/43, which is 2.33% a year, and the ladder was handing back 2.94%. Measured on the canonical plan the cash answer came out at 754,797 against the 932,072 a cash pot actually needs, 19% short, and LOWER than the invested figure beside it, directly under a panel telling the reader cash costs a multiple of investing. `buildPlan` now picks its own basis (`required.basis` / `required.target`): spend-down for a cash-only glide, safe rate for anything invested. It lives in the plan rather than in the table because the milestones, the earliest-retirement solver and the grid all read that one target and would otherwise disagree with each other.
  **The cash column is priced at the CAUTIOUS reading of cash, and that is what makes it comparable rather than rigged.** Once the basis was fixed, a second inconsistency surfaced: the grid zeroed cash's return and the Assumptions preset used its long run 0.9% real, so one reader got two answers for "what if I do not invest", and on the preset path cash came out needing LESS than investing. That inversion is not a bug in the arithmetic, it is an apples-to-oranges comparison: the invested column is a rate built to survive the worst run in the record, so pricing cash at its average puts an expected case beside a worst case. Cash has a bad run of its own and it is a decade of high inflation eating the nominal return, which is exactly zero real, so the grid holds it there and the caption says why. The fee went with it, because nobody pays a platform charge on a savings account, and leaving it in was what let the two paths disagree in the first place. On the preset path the reader prices cash themselves and the assumption line names the rate they chose rather than claiming zero.
  **The spending layers count guaranteed income, and a layer's bar is as tall as what it actually gets.** Both were wrong in the first version and both were quiet. The tiers were shares of the NET figure the pot has to find, so "Essentials" meant 58% of what the pot provides rather than of what the reader spends, which hid the most reassuring fact available: a state pension already covers most of the bottom layer, and that is why a bad year reaches the holidays and never the food. And every bar was sized off its FULL amount with only the printed figure changing, so a crash drew an unchanged wall of blocks with smaller numbers in it, which is the one reading the panel exists to produce. The scale is the fully funded stack and deliberately does not move with the slider, or nothing would ever appear to shrink.
  **A panel behind a fold mounts with whatever the plan said when it was first scrolled to, and then goes stale.** The bridge opens on the years between stopping and the pension starting; bring the retirement age forward afterwards and it is still answering the old question with nothing on screen saying so. Re-syncing on every change would throw away what the reader typed there, so it says the two disagree and offers one press to catch up, the same shape as the planning age control. `Math.max(1, ...)` around the default was its own small bug: it turned the ordinary case of stopping exactly at pension age into a one year bridge instead of falling through to the worked example.
  **The reference data is derived, never a typed table per country.** There is one published basket, the UK's, moved onto each country's prices by a comparative price level at a fixed reference exchange rate; 23 hand typed baskets would be 23 numbers nobody can check that drift apart on the first edit. The rate is deliberately not live, because a thirty year plan re-priced every fifteen seconds is noise dressed as precision. Every reference figure is approximate, a year or two old, named with its source on the page, and editable. `drawdown` had to be renamed `spendDown`: it is on the banned vocabulary list and `reader-copy.test.ts` reads identifiers too.

- **A SUB-TAB WRITES THE ADDRESS FROM THE PRESS, NEVER FROM AN EFFECT, AND THE VERSION THAT DID SILENTLY BROKE EVERY DEEP LINK IN THE APP.** Both rooms with sub-tabs had the same shape: read `?labtab=` / `?growthtab=` once on mount, hold the tab in state, and mirror that state back into the address from a `useEffect`. It reads as obviously correct and it destroys the very parameter it exists to honour. The read happens in a LAYOUT effect (`useHydratedCache`, which is right, and is what keeps the server's markup and the client's first render in agreement) and the mirror in a PASSIVE one, and nothing orders the re-render from the first ahead of the second: the mirror fires while the tab is still the server's fallback and writes that fallback over the reader's parameter. React's development double-invoke then re-reads the clobbered address and makes the wrong tab permanent, which is what turns a race into a reproducible bug. **Measured against the running app**: `?labtab=risk`, `?labtab=trends` and `?labtab=seasonality` every one landed on The mix with the address rewritten to `labtab=alloc`, and `?growthtab=retirement` landed on the compound calculator, the address carrying `retirement` at 500ms and `compound` by two seconds. Lab had shipped that way for months with this file documenting the opposite. **Writing from the press removes the race instead of sequencing it**: a deep link is read once and then left alone, and the address only ever changes because somebody chose a tab. Two details that are not optional. `replaceState` rather than `push`, so a sub-tab press does not pile onto the back stack the way a room change does. And `window.history.state` is **passed through**, because the App Router keeps its own routing state in there and replacing it with `null` leaves Back and Forward working from an entry the router no longer recognises. `sub-tab-links.test.ts` fails on a `searchParams.set` for either parameter inside a `useEffect` body, and on a `replaceState(null`. It reads text, so a mirror moved into a helper is beyond it; what it catches is the shape somebody reaches for first, which is the shape that was there. **The general lesson, which is this file's own rule in a new place: when one effect reads a source of truth and another writes to it, they are racing, and the fix is to delete one of them rather than to order them.**
